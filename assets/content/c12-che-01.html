Volumetric Analysis — the chemistry of measuring concentrations by measuring volumes. The most practical chapter of NEB Class 12 Physical Chemistry: its ideas run your entire lab work and it reliably appears in board exams as definitions, short conceptuals and one guaranteed numerical.

1. Chapter Overview

What this chapter teaches you. Chemists constantly need one answer: "exactly how much of this substance is present?" When that question is answered by reacting the unknown with a solution of known concentration and carefully measuring the volumes involved, the method is called volumetric analysis. This chapter builds every tool the method needs — concentration units (normality, molarity), standard solutions, indicators and the famous titration equation.

Why it matters in NEB exams. Expect two-mark definitions (primary/secondary standard, end point vs equivalence point), a conceptual on indicators, and at least one numerical using N₁V₁ = N₂V₂. Your entire Class 12 practical file — preparing N/10 Na₂CO₃, standardising HCl and KMnO₄ — is this chapter in action.

2. Important Definitions

NEB short questions lift their wording straight from these lines. Learn each as one clean sentence.

Qualitative analysis

Analysis that detects which constituents are present in a substance — identification, not amount.

Quantitative analysis

Analysis that measures how much of each constituent is present in a substance.

Volumetric analysis

Quantitative analysis in which the amount of an unknown constituent is determined by measuring the volumes of reacting solutions of known concentration.

Gravimetric analysis

Quantitative analysis in which the amount of a constituent is determined by weighing — usually the precipitate formed in the reaction.

Standard solution

A solution whose concentration (strength) is accurately known.

Primary standard substance

A substance from which a standard solution can be prepared directly by accurate weighing. Conditions: high purity, stable in air, not hygroscopic or deliquescent, reasonably soluble, and a fairly high equivalent weight. Examples: anhydrous Na₂CO₃, oxalic acid, K₂Cr₂O₇.

Secondary standard substance

A substance whose solution cannot be prepared directly by weighing — it must first be standardised against a primary standard. Examples: NaOH, HCl, KMnO₄, FeSO₄.

Normality (N)

The number of gram equivalents of solute dissolved per litre of solution.

Molarity (M)

The number of moles of solute dissolved per litre of solution.

Titration, equivalence point & end point

Titration: controlled addition of one solution from a burette to another until reaction is just complete. Equivalence point: the theoretical stage where exactly equal gram equivalents have mixed. End point: the experimentally observed colour change marking it — an ideal titration makes the two coincide.

Indicator

A tiny amount of dye added to the flask that changes colour sharply near the equivalence point, signalling the end point (e.g. methyl orange, phenolphthalein).

3. Key Concepts Explained Simply

3.1 Concentration is just a recipe ratio

"Concentrated" and "dilute" are vague words. Chemistry needs exact recipes: how many counting-units of solute per litre of solution. The two units differ only in what they count:

  • Mole-counting → Molarity: moles per litre — best when reactions count molecules.
  • Charge-counting → Normality: gram equivalents per litre — best for titrations, because acids and bases react equivalent-to-equivalent, not molecule-to-molecule.

One mole of H₂SO₄ supplies two moles of H⁺ — so one "mole" of it does the work of two "equivalents". That single fact creates every relation between N and M in this chapter.

3.2 Why some substances can't be weighed for standards

A standard solution demands that the mass you weigh equals exactly the mass you dissolved. Substances fail this test when they absorb moisture from air (hygroscopic/deliquescent, like solid NaOH), react with air (NaOH takes in CO₂ forming Na₂CO₃ on its surface), or slowly change composition. Hence the two-tier system: weigh a trustworthy primary standard, then use it to calibrate a secondary standard by titration — that calibration is called standardisation.

3.3 How a titration actually works

  1. Pipette a fixed volume of the unknown into a conical flask; add 1–2 drops of indicator.
  2. Fill the burette with standard solution; note the initial reading.
  3. Add titrant slowly, swirling; near the end add drop-by-drop until the colour just changes → note final reading.
  4. Volume used (the titre) = final − initial. Repeat until concordant.
  5. Apply N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ to find the unknown normality.

3.5 n-factor — why equivalent weight can depend on the medium

The n-factor is how many H⁺ an acid donates, OH⁻ a base accepts, total positive charge a salt splits into, or electrons a redox agent exchanges per formula unit. Since:

Equivalent weight = molecular mass ÷ n-factor

…anything that changes the electron count changes the equivalent weight. KMnO₄ is the classic case: Mn goes from +7 to +2 in acidic medium (5 electrons), to +4 in neutral (3 electrons) and to +6 in alkaline (1 electron):

KMnO₄ (M = 158): acidic → E = 158/5 = 31.6; neutral → E = 158/3 ≈ 52.7; alkaline → E = 158/1 = 158. Same salt, three different strengths — always note the medium before choosing E.

5. Derivations (relations proved step-by-step)

D1 — Normality straight from its definition

N is gram equivalents per litre, and geq = w/E:

N = (w/E) ÷ V(L) = w × 1000 ÷ (E × VmL)

Rearranged for the mass needed to prepare a given volume: w = N × V × E ÷ 1000 — the working equation of every "prepare N/10 solution" practical.

D2 — The bridge between normality and molarity: N = n × M

Moles of solute = w/M₀ and gram equivalents = w/E. Since E = M₀/n:

w/E = n × w/M₀, i.e. gram equivalents = n × moles.

Dividing both sides by volume in litres gives:

N = n × M

e.g. 0.1 M H₂SO₄ → 0.2 N (n=2); 0.1 M NaOH → 0.1 N (n=1)

D3 — Proof of the titration law N₁V₁ = N₂V₂

At the equivalence point the reactants have mixed in exactly equal gram equivalents: geq₁ = geq₂.

But geq = N × V(litre) for any solution. Substituting:

N₁V₁ = N₂V₂

the /1000 cancels, so volumes may stay in mL

Note: this balances equivalents, not moles — which is exactly why it works even when the balanced equation has unequal coefficients, and why normality is preferred in titrations.

D4 — Equivalent weight of KMnO₄ in three media

Acidic: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (n = 5)
Neutral: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ (n = 3)
Alkaline: MnO₄⁻ + e⁻ → MnO₄²⁻ (n = 1)

E = 158/5 = 31.6  |  158/3 ≈ 52.7  |  158/1 = 158

acidic · neutral · alkaline respectively

6. Important Numerical Types

NEB numericals from this chapter fall into five repeating patterns. Master one example of each and any variant becomes routine.

Type 1 — Preparing a standard solution (your practical!)

Q: How would you prepare exactly 250 mL of N/10 Na₂CO₃ solution? What mass is required?

Solution: E(Na₂CO₃) = 53. Gram equivalents needed = (N × V)/1000 = (0.1 × 250)/1000 = 0.025.
w = geq × E = 0.025 × 53 = 1.325 g.
Method: weigh 1.325 g accurately → dissolve in distilled water → transfer completely into a 250 mL volumetric flask → make up to the mark and shake for uniformity.

Type 2 — Normality / molarity of a prepared solution

Q: 4.9 g of H₂SO₄ is dissolved to make 1 L of solution. Find its normality and molarity.

Solution: N = w/(E × V) = 4.9/(49 × 1) = 0.1 N. M = 4.9/(98 × 1) = 0.05 M. Cross-check with the bridge: N = n×M = 2 × 0.05 = 0.1 ✓

Type 3 — Straight titration: find the unknown normality

Q: 20 mL of an HCl solution requires 25 mL of 0.1 N NaOH for complete neutralisation. Find the normality and strength of the acid.

Solution: N(acid) × 20 = 0.1 × 25 → N = 0.125 N.
Strength = N × E = 0.125 × 36.5 = 4.56 g/L.

Type 4 — Percentage purity via titration (multi-step)

Q: 1.32 g of an impure Na₂CO₃ sample is dissolved and made up to 250 mL. Exactly 25 mL of this solution needs 24 mL of 0.1 N HCl. Find % purity.

Solution:
① Sample normality: N₁ = (24 × 0.1)/25 = 0.096 N
② Total geq in flask: (0.096 × 250)/1000 = 0.024
③ Pure mass: 0.024 × 53 = 1.272 g
④ Purity: (1.272 ÷ 1.32) × 100 ≈ 96.4%

Type 5 — Redox titration with KMnO₄ (mind the medium!)

Q: 25 mL of FeSO₄ solution is completely oxidised by 25 mL of 0.1 N KMnO₄ in acidic medium. Find the strength of FeSO₄ in g/L.

Solution: Equivalents balance: 0.1 × 25 = N(FeSO₄) × 25 → N = 0.1 N.
Acidic-medium FeSO₄: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻ so E = 152. Strength = N × E = 0.1 × 152 = 15.2 g/L.

Exam discipline: write the formula first, substitute with units, end with units. For redox sums, state the medium before choosing E. For titrations, identify which solution is "1" and which is "2" — then it's pure arithmetic.

7. Exam-Focused Questions (with model answers)

Short-answer questions (2 marks each)

Q1. What is a primary standard substance? Give two examples.
Answer: A substance from which a standard solution can be prepared directly by accurate weighing. It must be highly pure, stable in air, non-hygroscopic, and have a reasonably high equivalent weight. Examples: anhydrous Na₂CO₃ and oxalic acid.

Q2. Why is NaOH not used as a primary standard?
Answer: Solid NaOH rapidly absorbs moisture from the air and also takes in CO₂ forming Na₂CO₃ on its surface. Its weighed mass therefore never corresponds to pure NaOH, so its solution must first be standardised against a primary standard such as oxalic acid.

Q3. Differentiate between end point and equivalence point.
Answer: The equivalence point is the theoretical stage at which exactly equal gram equivalents of the two reactants have mixed. The end point is the experimental stage at which the added indicator changes colour. In an ideal titration the two coincide; any small difference is the titration error.

Q4. Why are only one or two drops of indicator added during titration?
Answer: Indicators are themselves weak acids or bases. In excess they consume some of the titrant and shift the end point, besides making their own colour change dull and hard to detect.

Q5. Why can KMnO₄ act as its own indicator in redox titrations?
Answer: In acidic medium, slightly excess KMnO₄ imparts a permanent pale pink colour to the colourless reaction mixture — no separate indicator is needed, so it is called a self-indicator.

Numerical-style board questions (try yourself)

Q6. Calculate the mass of oxalic acid crystals (H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O) needed to prepare exactly 500 mL of N/10 solution. (Ans: w = 0.1 × 500 × 63 ÷ 1000 = 3.15 g)

Q7. 12.5 mL of an HCl solution neutralises 20 mL of N/5 NaOH. Find its strength in g/L. (Ans: N = 0.32 → 0.32 × 36.5 ≈ 11.7 g/L)

Q8. Find the equivalent weight of KMnO₄ as an oxidising agent in (a) acidic, (b) strongly alkaline medium. (Ans: 31.6 and 158)

Pattern recognition for the exam hall: volumes of two solutions + one concentration given → jump straight to N₁V₁ = N₂V₂. Mass given + normality asked → use N = 1000w/(EV). KMnO₄ mentioned → fix the medium before touching E.

8. Very Short Revision Notes

  • Volumetric analysis finds amount by measuring volumes; gravimetric analysis weighs precipitates instead.
  • Standard solution = known strength. Primary: weighed directly (Na₂CO₃, oxalic acid). Secondary: standardise first (NaOH, HCl, KMnO₄).
  • E = molecular mass ÷ n-factor · geq = w/E · N = geq per litre · M = moles per litre.
  • N = n × M — H₂SO₄ n=2, NaOH n=1, Na₂CO₃ n=2.
  • Titration law: N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ (equal gram equivalents at equivalence point).
  • w = NVE/1000 · Strength (g/L) = N × E.
  • Key E values: HCl 36.5 · H₂SO₄ 49 · HNO₃ 63 · NaOH 40 · KOH 56 · Ca(OH)₂ 37 · Na₂CO₃ 53 · oxalic 45/63 · K₂Cr₂O₇ 49.
  • KMnO₄ E values: acidic 31.6 · neutral ≈52.7 · alkaline 158.
  • Methyl orange range 3.1–4.4 (acidic side); phenolphthalein 8.3–10 (basic side).
  • Strong–strong titration → either indicator; weak acid–strong base → PF; strong acid–weak base → MO.
  • End point = observed change; equivalence point = theory's equal-equivalents stage.
  • KMnO₄ is self-indicator in acidic medium; only 1–2 drops of other indicators.
