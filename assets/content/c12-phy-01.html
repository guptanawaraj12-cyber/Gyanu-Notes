1. Chapter Overview

What this chapter is about. Until now you have treated every object as a point. Real objects — wheels, fans, flywheels, planets — are extended bodies that can rotate. Rotational dynamics answers three questions: what makes a body start spinning (torque), what resists a change in its spin (moment of inertia), and what stays conserved while it spins (angular momentum).

Why it matters in NEB exams. This unit regularly supplies one long question (almost always a derivation: rod MOI, KE of rotation, or conservation of angular momentum) plus short conceptual questions and at least one easy numerical. Master the derivations here and you secure marks predictably.

What you must be able to do after this chapter:

  • Define moment of inertia, radius of gyration, torque, angular momentum — in one clean line each.
  • Derive I for a uniform rod about both standard axes by integration.
  • Derive rotational KE = ½Iω², τ = Iα, W = τθ and P = τω from first principles.
  • State and explain conservation of angular momentum with real examples.
  • Solve numericals mixing ω–θ–t, energy, and L-conservation.

Prerequisite: Class 11 circular motion (ω, centripetal acceleration) — this chapter reuses those ideas constantly.

2. Important Definitions

Learn each of these as a single memorisable sentence — NEB short questions lift their wording directly.

Rigid body

A body whose particles keep fixed distances between themselves; it does not deform while moving. Every particle of a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis has the same angular velocity ω.

Moment of inertia (I)

The inability of a body to change its state of rest or uniform rotation by itself — i.e. the inertia of a body in rotational motion. Numerically, I = Σmr², the sum of each particle's mass times the square of its distance from the axis. Unit: kg·m².

Radius of gyration (k)

The perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation to a point where the whole mass of the body may be supposed to be concentrated so that I = Mk². It measures how far, on average, the mass sits from the axis. Unit: metre.

Torque (τ)

The turning effect of a force about an axis: τ = rF sin θ, the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the axis to its line of action (moment arm). Unit: N·m.

Angular momentum (L)

The quantity of rotational motion possessed by a body: L = Iω, the rotational analogue of linear momentum mv. Unit: kg·m²/s.

Conservation of angular momentum

If no net external torque acts on a system, its total angular momentum remains constant: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂.

3. Key Concepts Explained Simply

3.1 Why do we need "moment of inertia" at all?

Push an empty trolley and it moves; push a loaded one and it resists. That resistance is mass. Now spin two wheels of the same mass — one a light-rimmed ring, one with the weight near the axle. The ring is much harder to start and stop spinning. Mass alone can't explain this; what matters is how far that mass sits from the axis. Moment of inertia captures exactly that: I = Σmr². Because of the r², doubling a particle's distance quadruples its contribution. This is why a flywheel puts its rim far out, and why a figure skater spins faster just by folding her arms inward.

3.2 Torque is force's turning twin

To slide a crate you need force. To swing a door you need force applied the right way: far from the hinge and pushing across it. The same push near the hinge does almost nothing. Torque bundles those three intuitions into one number:

τ = r F sin θ

r = distance from axis · θ = angle between r and F
  • Push perpendicular, at maximum distance → biggest τ.
  • Push along the line through the axis (θ = 0°) → τ = 0, no turn at all.
  • A pair of equal-and-opposite forces whose lines don't meet forms a couple, which produces pure rotation with zero net force.

3.3 Newton's laws, translated to rotation

Every linear law has a rotational twin. Replace force F by torque τ, mass m by moment of inertia I, acceleration a by angular acceleration α, momentum mv by angular momentum Iω:

Linear motionRotational motion
v = u + atω = ω₀ + αt
s = ut + ½at²θ = ω₀t + ½αt²
v² = u² + 2asω² = ω₀² + 2αθ
F = maτ = Iα
p = mvL = Iω
K = ½mv²K = ½Iω²
W = Fs · P = FvW = τθ · P = τω

If you remember this table, half the chapter is already memorised — every derivation below is just one row being proved.

Derivation 1 — Relation between linear and angular quantities: v = rω

A particle at distance r from the axis moves along a circle. When the body turns through angle θ (in radians), the arc length travelled is s = rθ.

Differentiating with respect to time (r is constant):

ds/dt = r·dθ/dt  ⟹  v = rω

Differentiating again gives the tangential acceleration at = rα. This little relation is used inside almost every later proof, so it comes first.

Derivation 2 — τ = Iα (Newton's second law for rotation)

Consider one particle of mass m at distance r in a rigid body rotating with angular acceleration α. Its tangential acceleration is a = rα.

Newton's second law on this particle: F = ma = m(rα).

The torque of this force about the axis: τ = rF = r(mrα) = mr²α.

Every particle shares the same α. Summing over all particles:

Στ = Σmr²α = (Σmr²) α = I α

since Σmr² = I

Conclusion: net torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration — the exact rotational image of F = ma. A given torque produces less α for a larger I.

Derivation 3 — Kinetic energy of a rotating body: K = ½Iω²

Let the rigid body rotate with angular velocity ω. A particle m₁ at distance r₁ moves with speed v₁ = r₁ω (all points share the same ω).

Its kinetic energy: K₁ = ½m₁v₁² = ½m₁(r₁ω)² = ½m₁r₁²ω².

Total kinetic energy is the sum over all particles:

K = ½m₁r₁²ω² + ½m₂r₂²ω² + … = ½ (Σ m r²) ω²

every particle shares the same ω, so it comes out of the sum

K = ½ I ω²

rotational kinetic energy of a rigid body

Note for exams: write the sum symbol (Σ) explicitly before collapsing it into I — examiners award marks for that step.

Derivation 4 — Moment of inertia of a thin uniform rod about a perpendicular axis through its centre: I = Ml²/12

Let the rod have mass M and length l, with axis YY′ through the centre C, perpendicular to its length.

Mass per unit length = M/l. Take a tiny element of length dx at distance x from C. Its mass: dm = (M/l)dx.

Its contribution to I about the axis: dI = dm·x² = (M/l)x²dx.

Integrate x from −l/2 to +l/2 to cover the whole rod:

I = (M/l) ∫−l/2l/2 x² dx = (M/l)[x³/3] from −l/2 to l/2

I = (M/l) [x³/3]−l/2+l/2 = (M/l) ( l³/24 + l³/24 ) = (M/l) · l³/12

evaluating the limits: (l/2)³/3 = l³/24

I = Ml²/12

axis through centre, ⊥ to length

Derivation 5 — Same rod about an axis through one end: I = Ml²/3

Same setup, but now integrate x from 0 to l (the axis passes through end A):

I = (M/l) ∫0l x² dx = (M/l) [x³/3]0l = (M/l) · l³/3

evaluating the limits: l³/3 − 0 = l³/3

I = Ml²/3

axis through one end, ⊥ to length

Shortcut check: by the parallel-axis theorem Iend = Icm + Md² = Ml²/12 + M(l/2)² = Ml²/12 + Ml²/4 = Ml²/3 ✓ — quoting this cross-check in exams earns credit.

Derivation 6 — Work and power in rotational motion: W = τθ and P = τω

Let a force act on a particle of the body at distance r from the axis, directed along its tangent. When the body turns through a small angle dθ, that particle moves along its arc by ds = r dθ.

Work done on this one particle: dW = F ds = F (r dθ) = (r F) dθ = τ dθ, since the torque of the tangential force is τ = rF.

For a constant torque turning the whole body through a total angle θ, summing over every particle gives:

W = τ θ

work done by a constant torque — the rotational twin of W = Fs

Power is the rate of doing work. Differentiating W = τθ with respect to time (τ constant):

P = dW/dt = τ dθ/dt = τ ω

instantaneous power — the rotational twin of P = Fv

Work–energy check: using τ = Iα and ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ, W = τθ = Iαθ = ½Iω² − ½Iω₀² — exactly the change in rotational kinetic energy. Quoting this cross-check in the exam earns extra credit.

Derivation 7 — Angular momentum L = Iω and its conservation

A particle m₁ at r₁ has linear momentum p₁ = m₁v₁ = m₁r₁ω. Its angular momentum about the axis is L₁ = p₁r₁ = m₁r₁²ω.

Summing over all particles: L = Σmr²ω = ω Σmr² (ω is same for every particle).

L = I ω

angular momentum of a rigid body

Differentiate: dL/dt = d(Iω)/dt = I(dω/dt) = Iα = τ.

So τ = dL/dt — torque is the rate of change of angular momentum, exactly like F = dp/dt.

If the net external torque is zero (τ = 0), then dL/dt = 0, hence:

I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ = constant

law of conservation of angular momentum
Three classic illustrations (learn any two for the exam):
① Spinning skater: arms pulled in → I decreases → ω increases so Iω stays fixed; she spins faster without any push.
② Diver: tucking the body reduces I mid-air, increasing spin rate for somersaults.
③ Planets: a planet moves faster near the Sun (smaller r) — Kepler's second law is L-conservation in disguise.

6. Important Numerical Types

NEB numericals from this chapter fall into five repeating patterns. Master one example of each and you can solve any variant.

Type 1 — ω–θ–t kinematics (easiest marks)

Example: A flywheel initially at rest gains an angular acceleration of 4 rad/s². Find its angular velocity and angular displacement after 5 s, and the number of turns completed.

Solution: ω = ω₀ + αt = 0 + 4×5 = 20 rad/s.
θ = ω₀t + ½αt² = 0 + ½×4×5² = 50 rad.
Turns = θ/2π = 50/(2×3.1416) ≈ 7.96 ≈ 8 turns.

Pattern to recognise: "starts from rest / constant acceleration" → use the three rotational equations exactly like linear ones.

Type 2 — Unit conversion: rpm ↔ rad/s

Example: A wheel rotates at 300 rpm. Find ω in rad/s.

Solution: ω = 2πn/60 = 2π(300)/60 = 10π ≈ 31.4 rad/s.

Pattern: any question giving "revolutions per minute" — convert first, always.

Type 3 — Rotational kinetic energy

Example: A disc of mass 2 kg and radius 0.5 m spins at 10 rad/s about its central axis. Find I and K.

Solution: I = ½MR² = ½×2×0.25 = 0.25 kg·m². K = ½Iω² = ½×0.25×100 = 12.5 J.

Pattern: pick the correct MOI from the six-value list, then one substitution.

Type 4 — Conservation of angular momentum

Example: A skater spins at 2 rad/s with I = 6 kg·m². She pulls her arms in and I becomes 2 kg·m². Find her new angular velocity and compare KE.

Solution: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ → 6×2 = 2×ω₂ → ω₂ = 6 rad/s.
K₁ = ½×6×4 = 12 J; K₂ = ½×2×36 = 36 J → KE triples. The extra energy comes from her muscles doing work pulling the arms in.

Pattern: any "arms in / child walks on merry-go-round" problem — write I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ before anything else.

Type 5 — Work done by torque / power

Example: An engine applies a constant torque of 50 N·m while a shaft turns through 20 rad. Find the work done; if this took 4 s, find the power.

Solution: W = τθ = 50×20 = 1000 J. P = W/t = 250 W (equivalently P = τω with ω = 20/4 = 5 rad/s → 50×5 = 250 W). ✓ both routes agree.

7. Exam-Focused Questions (with model answers)

Short-answer questions (2–3 marks each)

Q1. Why is it easier to open a door by pushing at its outer edge, perpendicular to the door?
Answer: Torque τ = rF sin θ is the turning effect of force. At the outer edge r is maximum and pushing perpendicular gives sin θ = 1, so τ is maximum for the same applied force — producing the largest angular acceleration of the door.

Q2. Define radius of gyration and write its physical significance.
Answer: It is the perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation to a point at which the whole mass of the body may be considered concentrated so that I = Mk². Physically it measures how far the body's mass is, on average, distributed from the axis: a larger k means mass lies farther out and the body resists rotational change more strongly.

Q3. Why does a solid sphere roll down an incline faster than a ring of the same mass and radius?
Answer: For rolling, a = g sin θ / (1 + I/MR²). The ring has I = MR² while the sphere has I = (2/5)MR², so the sphere's denominator is smaller and its acceleration larger. Less of the sphere's mass rotates far from its axis, so a smaller share of the potential energy goes into rotation and more into forward speed.

Q4. A planet moves faster when closer to the Sun. Explain using angular momentum.
Answer: The Sun's gravitational pull acts along the line joining their centres, so external torque about the Sun is zero and L = mvr stays constant. As orbital radius r decreases near the Sun, v must increase to keep L constant.

Long questions (derivations — 5+ marks)

Q5. Show that the kinetic energy of a rotating rigid body is ½Iω². → Write Derivation 3 above in full.

Q6. Derive an expression for the moment of inertia of a uniform rod about an axis through one end perpendicular to its length. → Derivation 5 (add Derivation 4 as the centre case if asked "about both axes").

Q7. State and prove the law of conservation of angular momentum. Give one example. → Derivation 7 + any skater/diver/planet illustration.

Q8. Prove τ = Iα. → Derivation 2.

Q9. Derive expressions for the work done and the power developed in rotational motion. → Derivation 6.

Answer-writing tips: start derivations by defining every symbol; keep the Σ step visible before replacing it with I; draw the rod-with-axis sketch for MOI questions (1 mark is often for the figure); end numericals with units boxed in the final answer.
